# The Meter That Counted Sins in Kerosene

The notice above the Debt Meter was straightforward: *All Debts Must Be Measured Before Forgiven – Civic Ordinance 47-B.* But the meter itself had other ideas. Its cracked face flickered as I pressed my thumb to its surface, the numbers for Mrs. Varga’s water debt jumping from 12.50 to 98.76 and back again, like it was trying to remember which timeline it belonged to.  

I wiped the screen with my sleeve. “Still says 12.50,” I told Mrs. Varga, who glared at me like I’d invented drought. She smelled of lavender air freshener and quiet resentment, the kind that builds when your neighbor’s lawn stays green while yours cracks like a wishbone.  

“You tell your brother I need the sprinkler fixed *today*,” she said, leaning over the counter. Her necklace—a silver chain with a tiny shovel—bounced against the meter’s dented housing. “Or I’m calling the Council.”  

I nodded. My brother, Leo, had promised to fix the sprinklers three weeks ago. But Leo was busy being “consulted” by a tech startup in Seoul (his word, not mine; he’d never left Ohio). Mom visited tomorrow, and we’d agreed to let her believe Leo’s LinkedIn wasn’t a hallucination.  

At lunch, Sam found me eating dry crackers by the loading dock. He’d started bringing me snacks since the thing with his divorce—sometimes almonds, sometimes candy bars melted into their wrappers. “You’re supposed to *eat*,” he said, sliding a pear onto my clipboard. “Not just hold the fruit hostage.”  

I almost told him about Leo. About how the meter had shown me a version of myself last week where I’d left the Civic Hub to manage a farm stand, where debts were measured in tomatoes and forgiveness was free. Instead, I said, “Mrs. Varga thinks I’m a plumber.”  

Sam snorted. “You’re a *sinner*,” he said, grinning. “The meter proved it. Remember? ‘Unpaid guilt: 87%.’”  

The thing about the Debt Meter: it didn’t just tally money. When it glitched, it spat out debts you didn’t know you owed—emotional currency, old grudges, the weight of almost-lives. Sam’s divorce had shown up as a negative number (-$43.12), which we’d both laughed at until his eyes got wet.  

At 3 p.m., Mr. Gao sauntered in, his usual smugness dialed to 11. “Meter’s broken again,” he declared, slapping a citation on the counter. “It says I owe the city 14 pounds of bacon.”  

I swallowed. The screen *was* glitching—letters scrambling into hieroglyphs. “Let me recalibrate,” I muttered, thumbing the side panel. Sometimes, if I pressed just below the crack, it stabilized.  

But today, the screen flashed: *YOUR BROTHER’S LIE = 72 HOURS UNTIL DISCOVERY*.  

Mr. Gao peered over my shoulder. “Ohhh, juicy,” he whispered. “How much does Mom pay you to keep Leo’s ‘business trips’ quiet? I’ll trade you my bacon debt for the truth.”  

I froze. Sam, eavesdropping from the next booth, stiffened.  

“Outside,” Mr. Gao said, winking. “I’ve got a proposal.”  

He followed me into the alley, where the recycling bins overflowed with shredded citations. “I know about the meter’s… extras,” he said. “Heard it sing once. Sounded like my grandma’s voice. You scratch my back, I’ll scratch yours. Reduce my debt, and I won’t tell anyone your brother’s a fraud.”  

I could’ve threatened him. But the meter’s warning pulsed behind my eyes. Seventy-two hours. Mom’s visit was in 48.  

“You want a favor?” I said. “Fine. But you’re going to help me ‘misplace’ the new meter they’re installing tomorrow.”  

The city had been threatening to replace our broken relic with a sleek glass tablet—no glitches, no forgiveness. No way to bury debts in alternate numbers.  

Mr. Gao grinned. “Now you’re speaking my language.”  

That night, I found Sam waiting by my car. “You’re protecting your brother,” he said, not a question. “Why?”  

“Because he’s *my* almost-life,” I said. “The one where I didn’t take this job, where I left.”  

The meter had shown me that version too. Sam and I in a farmers’ market, laughing.  

He touched my wrist. “What if we’re both possible?”  

But the next morning, the new meter arrived—a pristine white slab. By noon, Mr. Gao “accidentally” spilled coffee on it, and I used the distraction to smash its circuitry with a staple remover.  

When the supervisor yelled, I didn’t flinch. Let them fire me. Let the debts be unforgivable.  

At 5 p.m., Mom called. “Leo’s here,” she said. “He’s doing wonderfully.”  

I pictured her face, smooth with belief.  

That night, Sam brought me a melon. “You’re still a sinner,” he said.  

I smiled. “But I’m *your* sinner.”  

The old meter hummed in the corner, its cracked face soft with sympathy. It wouldn’t last forever. But for today, it was enough.
