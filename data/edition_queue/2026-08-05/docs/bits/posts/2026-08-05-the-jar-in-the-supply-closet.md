# The Jar in the Supply Closet

Mabel stampede REJECTED across the mason jar’s label as she listened to Mrs. Halvorsen complain about the new forms. The jar sat on the shelf behind her, half-buried in stale instant coffee packets. Its paper label had read *Preserves* that morning. Now it throbbed with red ink, the letters lopsided as a teenager’s diary entry.  

“You people never make anything *simple*,” Mrs. Halvorsen snapped, waving a clipboard. Her chinchilla collar shed fluff onto the counter. “My husband’s discharge papers should’ve been ready last Thursday.”  

Mabel kept her smile pinned at a 45-degree angle, the same tilt she used when the radiator hissed during tenant meetings. “Let me check the backlog, Mrs. Halvorsen. There’s been… irregularities.”  

The jar had started changing the week they fired her predecessor, Carl. He’d left behind the jar, a transistor radio that only picked up farm reports, and a memo titled *Contingency Protocols for Temporal Displacement (Draft)* in his desk drawer. Mabel hadn’t read it. She’d been temping for six months; reading memos felt like claiming ownership of a borrowed coat.  

That afternoon, the jar labeled itself *EVIDENCE*. The ink bled into a sketch of a wilting dahlia. Mabel noticed when she fetched her lunch—a crusty spinach turnover from the cafeteria that tasted more of asbestos than pastry. The supply closet smelled of mildew and pencil shavings. Carl used to keep a geranium on the windowsill. It had died the day they cleared his desk, petals curling like shoji screens.  

“Rules are rules,” Mabel said later to a man whose overcoat smelled of wet dog and pickled herring. He’d come to dispute a fine for parking his horse-drawn cart in the no-standing zone. “But maybe we can… adjust the timeline.” She didn’t mean to say it. The jar had just flipped to *NEGOTIABLE*.  

He squinted. “You’re the new one. Carl never gave me any lip about the cart.”  

“Carl’s gone,” Mabel said. The jar shuddered. A new line appeared: *HE NEVER TOLD YOU ABOUT THE HORSES, DID HE?*  

Mabel’s aunt had kept a jar like this. Or maybe it was a different Mabel. Some mornings, the names on the cubicles shifted. *D. Mabel* or *M. Dabel* or just an asterisk. The office roared with printers and the squeak of janitorial carts. No one noticed the jar except the intern, Kevin, who’d started two weeks ago and already treated the filing cabinets like old friends.  

“Why’s that jar always changing?” he asked, peering into the closet.  

“You see that?” Mabel froze. Most people didn’t. Carl had said only those “in the flow” would notice. He’d been wearing his coat and holding a suitcase when he told her, the day before he vanished. “Just a draft, Mabel. You’ll know when it’s your turn.”  

Kevin leaned in. The jar now read *TRUST NO ONE OVER 30*. The letters were made of tiny beetles crawling in formation.  

“Antique,” Mabel said. “Part of the building’s… historical record.”  

At home, Mabel’s roommate’s cat coughed up a hairball that looked exactly like Carl’s mustache. She dreamed of dahlia roots twisting through filing cabinets, pulling documents into the soil.  

The next morning, the jar was empty. Just a clean label: *READY*.  

Mrs. Halvorsen returned with a lawyer. The jar now sat on Mabel’s desk, brazen as a stapler.  

“I’ve been advised,” the lawyer said, “that your office has been processing my client’s claim in 1987 dollars.”  

Mabel didn’t blink. The jar hummed. She’d practiced this. “There was a misalignment. We’re correcting it.”  

“How?”  

She turned the jar toward them. The label now read *THE HORSES KNOW*. The lawyer’s eyes glazed. Mrs. Halvorsen gasped, as if recognizing an old photograph.  

“Carl used to say that,” she whispered. “When he’d… skip days.”  

Mabel nodded. The jar’s ink dissolved into a sketch of a clock face with no hands.  

“Let’s start over,” she said.  

Outside, the first dahlia of spring pushed through the snow by the fire escape. It bent toward the window, listening.  

The jar now reads *ARCHIVED* and hasn’t changed in weeks. Mabel still checks every morning. Kevin calls her “Chief” now. The radiator hisses less when she’s near.  

She keeps the window open for the dahlia.  

It’s not a promotion, but it’s a kind of staying.
