# The Token Window

I polished the token with my sleeve as the line grew. It was a dull bronze disc, warm from my pocket, stamped with a symbol no one could quite place—like a cross between a subway map and a Rorschach blot. The woman at the front of the line tapped her passport against the counter. “My son’s in the hospital,” she said. “The train to MedRow won’t let me board without clearance.” Her voice wavered, but her eyes were dry. I knew that look. The kind that meant she’d already cried enough to flood the entire transit system.  

The token hummed when I pressed it to the scanner. Not a sound, really, but a vibration that traveled up my arm. The screen flashed *NEED CONFIRMED*. I handed her the token. “Take the 14B,” I said. “It’ll let you through.” She didn’t ask how. Nobody did.  

My shift started at dawn. By noon, I’d processed a man with a violin case full of screws (he claimed he was a repairman; the token didn’t care), a teenager trying to reach a job interview in a district that technically didn’t exist anymore, and an old man who just wanted to sit in the waiting room because his knees hurt. The token decided who got through. Not me. Never me.  

My sister called during lunch. “You got my message about the deposit?” she asked. Our apartment complex had started locking the stairwells at night, “for security.” If we didn’t pay the new fee by midnight, they’d change the codes. Again.  

“I’ll handle it,” I said, staring at the lone token left in the tray. It glinted like it knew I was lying.  

The rules were clear: tokens were for transit emergencies only. My sister’s lockout wasn’t an emergency. Not yet. But the token didn’t ask questions. It didn’t care about deposits or sisterly obligations. It only cared about need.  

Mara showed up at 3 p.m., her uniform smelling of antiseptic. She worked the night shift at the med depot, swapping painkillers for favors. We’d been dancing around each other for months, exchanging coffee and half-smiles. “Got a token for me?” she asked. “My niece’s school bus got rerouted. She’s stuck at the East Hub.”  

The token vibrated in my palm. *NEED CONFIRMED*. I wanted to tell her I’d run out. That the last one was spoken for. But the words stuck. Instead, I slid it across the counter. “14B again,” I muttered.  

She pocketed it without asking how it worked. “Buy you dinner later?”  

I nodded. A lie. By dinner, the token would be gone, and so would my chance to fix the stairwell code.  

At 5 p.m., a boy no older than twelve pressed a crumpled note against the glass. “My dad’s in the slow lane,” he said. “They won’t move him without a clearance token.” His voice didn’t waver. Dry eyes. I recognized the look now.  

The token lay still in my hand. No hum. No flash. Just cold bronze. *NEED DENIED*.  

The boy didn’t flinch. “How much?” he asked.  

I froze. The system didn’t do bribes. The token didn’t take bribes. But the boy’s fingers drummed a rhythm only he could hear, a nervous staccato that matched the thud of my pulse.  

“I can’t,” I said.  

He left.  

At 6 p.m., I closed the window. The empty tray stared back. My sister’s voice played in my head: *You always think you can fix everything.*  

I took the long way home, past the shuttered kiosks and the coffee cart that still served lukewarm brew to commuters who no longer existed. The token burned in my pocket.  

At the apartment, I found my sister on the stairs, her shoulder propped against the door. “They changed the code,” she said. “I’ve been out here an hour.”  

I held out the token. It hummed.  

She didn’t ask where I got it.  

The next morning, the line was shorter. Mara didn’t show. The token tray stayed empty.  

I started polishing the counter instead.  

It didn’t hum. It didn’t have to.
