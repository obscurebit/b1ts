# The Trophy That Leaned

You’re supposed to pick up the trophy from the engraver by 3 PM. Not because it’s valuable—hell, it’s just a dented bike wheel mounted on a plaque—but because Marv insists it “calibrate the shop’s moral axis” every afternoon. The plaque reads *Most Improved* in peeling gold lettering, though no one remembers who won it or when. You hate the thing. Every time you dust it, you think of how Marv vanished three months ago, leaving you to manage the repair shop and its constellation of weirdos who bring in bikes with “ghost wobbles” and “emotional rust.”  

The errand goes wrong when you trip on the engraver’s threshold and the trophy’s plaque faceplants into a puddle. The words blur, letters swimming like ink in water. You blot it with a napkin, but now it reads *Most Emerged* or *Most Submerged*—hard to tell. The engraver shrugs. “Still a trophy,” he says.  

Back at the shop, you prop it on the counter as usual. The first customer, a woman with a flat tire and a toddler strapped to her back, eyes it. “That the new policy?” she asks. You don’t know what she means. The trophy leans toward her, ever so slightly, like it’s nodding. She blinks. “Alright then.” She leaves a five-dollar bill and a half-eaten granola bar as tip.  

This is how you learn the trophy’s secret: when it leans, people assume it’s endorsing their choices. The lean says, *Yes, this is how things are done now.* By 5 PM, you’ve used it to resolve three disputes—a guy insisting his cracked frame wasn’t his fault, a couple arguing over whether their tandem bike needed a “sympathy alignment”—and the shop’s earned more cash than all last week.  

But the lean starts failing at closing. The trophy goes rigid, plaque gleaming neutrally. A man arrives with a mangled e-bike and a story about a raccoon “hexing” his spokes. Without the lean, he scoffs. “Since when does a paperweight run this place?” He slams the door.  

You call Marv’s old number. It rings, then plays a voicemail: *“If you’re hearing this, I’ve finally achieved temporal submersion. Don’t bother replying.”*  

The next morning, the trophy leans again, but only toward children. A boy points. “It likes me!” His mother beams, pays double for a brake adjustment. You wonder if the trophy’s just amplifying something already there—desire to please, hunger for validation.  

By week’s end, the leans are erratic. The trophy favors the anxious, the overly polite, the ones who apologize for existing. Regulars grumble. Newcomers arrive, drawn by rumors of the “wise bike shrine.” You start hating the thing all over again.  

Then, during a lull, it leans toward you.  

Just you. The plaque’s words have bleached out entirely now, a blank silver slate. You stare. It leans harder, insistently, as if to say: *You’re part of this too. You’ve always been part of this.*  

You unplug the shop’s “Open” sign. You take the trophy down and stick it in the back room with the broken wheel truing stands and Marv’s moth-eaten sweater. You write a note: *Policy changed. No more leans.*  

When the first customer arrives, you tell her, “We fix bikes now. No extras.”  

She frowns but hands over her keys.  

The trophy stays in the back. It doesn’t lean. It doesn’t need to.  

You refuse to ask it what it wants.
