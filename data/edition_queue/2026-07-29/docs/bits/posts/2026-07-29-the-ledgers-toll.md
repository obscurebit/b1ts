# The Ledger's Toll

SCHEDULE ENTRY 14-B: All third-class passengers must present their transit vouchers before boarding. Failure to comply will result in immediate removal by station marshals.  

Harold Finch straightened the ledger on the counter, its cracked leather spine flaking like dried mud. The new fluorescent lights hummed above, too bright for a place that used to smell like ink and shoe leather. Now it smelled like disinfectant and something electric, burnt.  

“Finch!” Mabel hissed from the ticket window. She jabbed a thumb at the man in the too-shiny suit blocking the queue. “Your friend’s here.”  

Vinnie Lozowski. Harold’s debt, acquired three months prior when he’d forged a voucher for Mabel’s niece—tuberculosis, three kids, no other options. Vinnie had “helped” with the border checkpoint. Now he leaned against the podium, silk tie gleaming, and flicked a cigarette ash onto the polished floor.  

“Heartfelt condolences,” Vinnie said, though Harold’s mother had died six months ago. “Heard you’re acting supervisor. Real classy.” His eyes flicked to the ledger. “Busy day, Finch? Or can we discuss my little *investment*?”  

Harold’s ears burned. Mabel had called the forgery an act of kindness. He’d called it theft. They hadn’t spoken of it since.  

The ledger trembled. Pages fluttered to a stop at an entry stamped REDIRECTED. A column of names, each paired with a symbol: a drop of ink, a smudged fingerprint, a child’s drawing of a train.  

Vinnie’s smile thinned. “Clock’s tickin’, pal.”  

Harold placed a hand on the ledger. “What do you want to know?”  

“Cute.” Vinnie leaned in. “I want the name of the next freight heading to Buffalo. The one carryin’ the union’s cash.”  

The ledger shuddered. A line appeared beneath the last entry: *Cost: one button from your coat.*  

Harold stiffened. The top button of his vest was his grandfather’s, brass, engraved with a locomotive. He hesitated, then twisted it free. The ledger swallowed it with a sound like a closing door.  

The page turned. A new entry glowed: *Freight 217-B. Departs 3:14 PM. Guard carries a .38 special.*  

Vinnie grinned. “See? That wasn’t so hard.”  

Mabel watched from the window, arms crossed. When Vinnie left, whistling, she came over.  

“You didn’t,” she whispered.  

“He’ll come back,” Harold said. “They always do.”  

“You could’ve asked for help.”  

“And tell them what? That I’m running a train station with a magic ledger that eats buttons?”  

Mabel snorted. “It’s not magic. It’s a union thing. My uncle had one. Costs you something real, but it keeps the truth from getting watered down.”  

Harold stared at the ledger. The entry for Freight 217-B had bled slightly, ink staining the page like a bruise.  

At 3:14 PM, a woman in a frayed coat approached. Her voucher was forged, the edge too crisp. Harold’s chest tightened.  

“Problem?” she asked.  

He glanced at the ledger. It lay still.  

“No,” he said. “Just… hold that up to the light.”  

She obeyed. The watermark matched. He stamped it.  

As she walked away, Mabel raised an eyebrow. “Since when do you check watermarks?”  

Harold touched the ledger’s empty buttonhole. “Since I started paying attention.”  

The schedule entry above them buzzed, the fluorescent lights flickering. For a moment, the words blurred into something almost human: *All passengers must choose what they’re willing to lose.*  

Harold didn’t notice. He was watching the woman disappear down the platform, her shoulders uncurled for the first time.  

Mabel followed his gaze. “You’re getting soft.”  

“No,” Harold said. “I’m getting better at the job.”  

The ledger, silent now, kept its thoughts to itself.
