# The Badge That Anchored Time

A damp stain bloomed on the repair shop’s schedule, the kind that smelled like mildew and yesterday’s coffee. Neville had spilled again. Vera circled his name with a fingertip, the ink on the page blurring as her badge pulsed—a slow, green throb against her collarbone. The badge didn’t like delays.  

*Rule 4.2,* the instructional voice in her head droned, though she’d long since memorized the manual, *“Repeated use of discretionary waivers establishes binding precedent. Convenience breeds obligation.”*  

She ignored it. The voice was just the city’s way of outsourcing guilt.  

Neville limped into the shop, his overalls grease-stiffened, a mangled toaster clutched to his chest like a wounded animal. “Heating element’s out,” he said, as if she couldn’t see the frayed wires dangling like nerves.  

“You’re late,” Vera said, not unkindly. The badge flickered amber.  

“Apartment’s thermostat crapped out. Had to fix it before the pipes freeze.” He set the toaster down, its chrome surface fogging in the damp air. “You know how the super is. Won’t lift a finger unless there’s a puddle.”  

Vera’s badge thrummed. *Rule 3.7: “Custodial dependencies are nontransferable. Emotional appeals do not invalidate debt.”*  

She slid a receipt toward him. “Three outstanding balances. The toaster’s another twenty.”  

Neville’s jaw worked. “I fixed your dryer last week. And the intercom. And the—”  

“The badge doesn’t forget,” she said, hating the way his eyes dropped. The truth was, she’d let him owe for months, waiving fees here and there, each concession a pebble in the river of her resolve. Now the river was a dam.  

The shop door chimed. A woman in a salt-stained coat stepped in, holding a child’s rain boot. “Found this in the alley,” she said. “Thought maybe it’s yours?”  

Neville brightened. “Lila’s been missing that.”  

The woman smiled. “No charge for the return. But my sink’s been gurgling something awful. You free tonight?”  

Vera’s badge flared crimson. *Rule 1.1: “Precedent overrides discretion. Obligations compound.”*  

Neville opened his mouth, but Vera cut in. “He’s busy.”  

The woman blinked. “The board said he owes a favor. From… three years ago?”  

Neville paled. The favor had been small then—a leaky faucet fixed during a blackout, a kindness traded for a cup of sugar and a promise. But the badge had recorded it, and the badge did not forget.  

“I’ll do it,” Vera said quickly, before Neville could speak.  

The badge settled, green again. The woman left, the boot swinging from her fist like a pendulum.  

Neville stared. “You don’t owe her anything.”  

“No,” Vera said, “but you do.” She tore up the receipt. The toaster’s heating element would have to wait. Some debts, once acknowledged, became anchors.  

Outside, the rain started. The badge hummed, satisfied.  

Neville reached for her hand, but she pulled away, not unkindly. There were rules, and there was the damp, relentless truth of them.  

When he was gone, Vera pressed her badge to the schedule, letting it burn the forgiveness into the paper. The stain spread, softer now.  

She’d be cruel in small ways tomorrow. Today, she let the precedent break.
