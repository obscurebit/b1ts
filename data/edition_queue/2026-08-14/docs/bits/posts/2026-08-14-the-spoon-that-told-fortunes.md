# The Spoon That Told Fortunes

The first time the spoon lied, it was telling the truth.  

Maren didn’t know this yet. She only knew the ration ledger was short three ounces of lard, and the spoon—dented, nickel-plated, engraved with *Property of St. Agnes Orphan Asylum*—had just measured out a full portion to a boy with hollow cheeks and a habit of hiding bread under his mattress. “For your health,” she said, as she always did, and turned before he could thank her. Gratitude was a currency she couldn’t afford to accept.  

That was 1937. By 1941, the spoon’s lies had become infrastructure.  

The orphanage kitchen hummed with the arithmetic of want. The city’s allocation committee met monthly to adjust rations based on “nutritional efficiency,” a phrase Maren’s supervisor, Mr. Varga, spat like a curse. “They think we’re running a hotel,” he’d growl, scratching his chin fur where shavings of pencil graphite clung like dandruff. Maren said nothing. She was too busy balancing the books: subtracting the weight of the spoon’s occasional generosity, adding the grams it “misplaced” when the inspectors came.  

The spoon’s behavior was simple, if perverse. When Maren used it to measure food for a child who seemed on the verge of tears, it underreported by 10%. For children who stole or lied, it overreported by 15%, creating phantom surpluses that vanished by morning. No one questioned the discrepancies. Not the inspectors, who trusted the numbers because they trusted the institution. Not the older girls, who learned to watch Maren’s hands and time their coughs to mask the sound of extra oats hitting their bowls. Not even the boy—now a gaunt teenager with a scarred knee and a talent for fixing radios—who still hid food but had started leaving her apples from the tree outside, polished to a feverish shine.  

Then, in March 1941, the spoon stopped lying.  

Maren noticed during the morning ration. The metal felt colder than usual, its bowl unyieldingly accurate as it doled out precisely 2.8 ounces of margarine to a new arrival, a girl with a face like crumpled paper. The child stared at the measurement, then at Maren. “That’s not enough,” she said.  

“It is,” Maren said.  

“It’s not.”  

Maren’s throat tightened. She reached for the spoon again, but the girl was already walking away, her bowl clutched like a secret.  

By noon, the kitchen was in revolt. A boy with a broken finger (courtesy of a falling dictionary in the library) demanded his “usual extra.” A nurse, her apron stained with iodine and regret, hissed, “You’ve been slackening, Maren. We all saw it.” Even Mr. Varga, usually indifferent to the kitchen’s alchemy, appeared in the doorway, his shadow stretching like a tally mark across the floor. “The committee’s coming tomorrow,” he said. “Make sure the numbers… comport.”  

Maren waited until evening to test the spoon. In the storeroom, she measured and remeasured a bag of flour. Each time, the spoon gave the same precise weight. She pried open its bowl with a screwdriver, half-expecting to find a hidden compartment, a spring, anything. But it was just metal, warped and honest.  

The next morning, she found the boy—the radio boy—waiting by the apple tree. He held out a small, oil-stained envelope. “Found this in the workshop,” he said. Inside was a handwritten note, the ink smudged: *If you fix the spoon, we’ll fix the radio. —The Children.*  

Maren laughed, a sharp, startled sound. “You think I broke it?”  

He shrugged. “Or it broke you.”  

The inspection began at 10 a.m. Maren stood rigid as the committee chair, a woman with a hat like a ship’s prow, scrutinized the ledger. “Your numbers are… inconsistent,” the woman said. “Last month, you reported 12 pounds of sugar. This month, 12 pounds exactly. No waste. No error.”  

Maren nodded. “We’ve improved our methods.”  

The woman turned the page. “And the lard shortage?”  

“Resolved.”  

“Remarkable.” She closed the ledger. “You should be proud, Miss… ?”  

“Varga,” Maren said, lying for the first time in four years.  

When the committee left, Mr. Varga cornered her in the hallway. “You’re a clever girl,” he said, his breath smelling of peppermint and bourbon. “But cleverness isn’t a ration we track.”  

That night, Maren took the spoon to the workshop. The radio boy was there, soldering a wire with tongue-out concentration. “They want it fixed,” she said.  

He looked up. “They don’t get to want things.”  

“I know.” She placed the spoon on the bench. “But I do.”  

He picked it up, turned it, and grinned. “It’s not broken. It’s just… tired.”  

Maren waited.  

“So,” he said, “what do you want it to lie about?”  

The next morning, the spoon measured 3.2 ounces of lard for every child. By noon, the kitchen was happy, the ledger balanced, and the apple tree heavy with fruit.  

Maren never learned the boy’s name. She didn’t need to.  

The spoon, meanwhile, never stopped lying again.  

But then, neither did she.
