# The Chlorophyll Ledger

The first time the leaf-print receipt curled at my fingers and whispered *not you*, I mistook it for a glitch. The second, I pressed my palm harder to the bulletin board, as if insistence could rewrite the chlorophyll-inked rows. The third, I noticed Mrs. Halvorsen from 4B watching, her thumb hooked in the pocket of her upcycled jumpsuit, the one with the frayed hem she’d let me “accidentally” take from the laundry cart last solstice.  

The Market Cooperative’s new system was supposed to be fair: every household gets one harvest voucher per moon cycle, printed on recycled lettuce leaves treated with conductive sap. The leaves only reveal their numbers to the correct recipient. Or so the brochure claimed.  

I’d practiced my speech to the Clerk of Accounts for weeks. *My mother’s dialysis machine eats three liters of distilled water a day. We’ve paid our biomass tax. This is an emergency reallocation request.* My voice would waver just enough to sound pitiable, not scheming. But the Clerk never looked up from her desk, where she balanced a portable freezer the size of a child’s coffin, its lid ajar to reveal vials of something gelatinous and pulsing.  

“The schedule doesn’t recognize you,” she said on my seventh attempt, not unkindly. “It’s for the boy in 7A.”  

I knew the boy. Felix. Twelve, allergic to everything, kept a pet fungus in a teacup. His mother died in the last grid collapse, his father traded to a terraforming crew for debt. He had no one to rehearse speeches with.  

Mrs. Halvorsen cornered me by the hydroponic bay, where the tomatoes hummed their ripeness to anyone who’d listen. “You’re wasting your time,” she said. “The leaves don’t care about your mother. They care about the soil. Felix’s family’s been composting their dead for years. Built up the mycorrhizal network something fierce.”  

I didn’t know whether to believe her. The Cooperative encouraged “contributions to the biome,” but nobody talked about what that meant. Not since the incident with the Patel twins and the incinerator.  

That night, I slipped into the print room. The leaves waited in a plastic crate, still damp from the printer’s gut. I found Felix’s voucher easily—its edges glowed faintly, hungry. I palmed it, feeling the sap prickle my skin, and swapped it with a forged one I’d made from a rejected invoice.  

The next morning, Mrs. Halvorsen appeared at my door holding a withered leaf. “This was in the recycling,” she said. “Yours?”  

I took it. The numbers had bled into nonsense.  

At the clinic, my mother’s machine hissed its hourly complaint. I fed it the last of our water ration. The voucher in my pocket felt heavier every hour.  

Felix never came for his harvest.  

I ate the forged leaf myself, bitter as gall, and waited for the mycorrhizal network to notice.
