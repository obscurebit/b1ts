# The Altered Notice

The first lie of the day was a good one: Mrs. Peet from 4B claiming her cat hadn’t scratched the delivery boy, though the boy’s torn sleeve and the cat’s smug licking of its paw said otherwise. Evelyn stamped the complaint with a *Received* seal, her notary stamp dipping hard into the inkpad, the way her mother taught her. The notice board above the desk shuddered, its pinned papers rustling like dry leaves. Ink bled across the topmost form, rewriting the schedule for trash collection into a list of names, including Mrs. Peet’s, now marked *Account Settled*.  

Evelyn didn’t look at Marcus. He stood by the window, pretending to inspect the new storm shutters, his posture stiff as a schoolboy’s. The board hadn’t updated all week, not since the fire. Since the insurance adjuster asked too many questions about the charred remains of the old notice board, the one that had hung in their foyer for thirty years.  

“You charged her?” Marcus said finally, thumb hooked in his pocket, voice low.  

“Five dollars,” Evelyn said. “She could afford it.”  

“You didn’t ask me.”  

“You were busy.”  

He turned. His tie was loose, the way it got when he’d been pulling at it, nervous. The partnership papers between them were notarized, of course, but the romance was newer, fragrant as the lemon oil he’d used on the floors that morning. Evelyn hated how the smell mixed with the lingering smoke.  

The doorbell rang. A man in a wool coat too thick for April, holding a paper envelope. “Got a notice to post,” he said. “Landlord says I gotta file it here.”  

Evelyn took the envelope. Eviction notice. The man’s name was Kovac, according to the header, and his landlord was the same company that owned half the buildings on the block, including Evelyn and Marcus’s. She glanced at the notice board. It hummed faintly, like a refrigerator.  

“Standard fee,” she said.  

Kovac smiled. “I don’t pay fees. I pay rent.”  

Marcus cleared his throat. “Policy is—”  

“I know the policy,” Kovac interrupted. “Your board there? It’s got a reputation. People say if you lie right, it sorts things out.”  

Evelyn’s pen hovered. The board’s ink began to crawl, slow and deliberate, across the eviction notice in her hand.  

“I’m not lying,” Kovac said, which was true enough that the board didn’t react. He leaned forward. “But my landlord? He’s lying about the rent escrow. Says I didn’t pay April’s, but I’ve got the canceled check.”  

A polite lie, Evelyn thought. The kind that greased the wheels of commerce. She nodded. “Five dollars.”  

Marcus made a sound like a deflating tire.  

When Kovac left, the notice board thrashed, papers flapping. The eviction notice in Evelyn’s hand now read *Lease Renewed – No Penalty*. The landlord’s name was gone, replaced with *Settled*.  

Marcus gripped the desk. “We can’t keep doing this.”  

“You did it for Mrs. Peet.”  

“That was different. She’s old. Lonely.”  

“And Kovac’s not?”  

The phone rang. Evelyn answered. A woman’s voice: “Check your board. My husband’s name is off the delinquency list. Again.”  

Evelyn glanced at the board. The woman’s name was there, *Account Settled*, though she’d only filed yesterday.  

“Charging more,” the woman said, “is that the new rule?”  

Evelyn hung up. Marcus was watching the board, which had started to bleed ink onto the floor, a sticky puddle pooling beneath it.  

“We’re going to get audited,” he said.  

“We’re not,” Evelyn said. “The board sees to it.”  

He stepped closer. “It’s not supposed to *do* this. It’s supposed to—”  

“—update after lies?” she finished. “It’s adapting. Like we are.”  

His face tightened. The financial detail she never spoke aloud: the board’s magic had doubled their income, but the rent on the building had tripled. The company owned the lease, the insurance, the quiet men who came to ask about the fire.  

The doorbell rang again. A teenager, acne and ambition, holding a subscription form for the neighborhood watch. “My dad says you gotta post this.”  

Evelyn took it. The board shivered. She could charge the boy, or she could lie—say the form was incomplete, send him away. But Marcus was watching, his hand on the doorjamb, and she saw the moment he decided to intervene.  

“I’ll handle this,” he said, taking the form. “No charge today.”  

The boy left, grinning. The board went still.  

Evelyn unclipped her notary seal. “You’re making rules now?”  

“I’m keeping us afloat.”  

The ink puddle reached her shoe. She thought of the fire, how the old board had burned so fast, how the insurance money had paid for the new floors, the new shutters, Marcus’s tie. How the company had stopped by the next day, smiling, to “adjust” their lease.  

“I want proof,” she said quietly. “That we’re not just… their puppet.”  

Marcus laughed, bitter and bright. “You want proof? Check the board.”  

She did. Beneath the ink, beneath the names and schedules, a new line had appeared: *Partnership Dissolved*.  

The doorbell rang again. And again.  

Evelyn picked up the phone, dialed the company’s main office. “I need to speak to the landlord,” she said.  

Marcus stared. The board began to rewrite itself, fast and furious, names and numbers swirling.  

“I’ll take the next caller,” Evelyn said, and hung up.  

The notice board fell silent.  

Marcus reached for her hand. She pulled away.  

Outside, the line of supplicants grew.  

Evelyn smiled, small and sharp.  

“New rule,” she said. “Everyone pays double.”  

The board hissed approval.
