# The Responsibility Box

“You’re saying we should let the east wing collapse so we can afford new radiators?” Mrs. Peet’s voice stayed level, but she gripped the ring box like it might bolt. Its silver surface, engraved with daisies that seemed to wilt when no one was looking, had warmed to her palm. *Mine for now*, she thought. *But not if I falter.*  

“Not collapse,” said Mr. Halvorsen, the landlord’s proxy, adjusting his string tie. “*Settle.* It’s an old building. Letting go of one wall eases pressure on the rest.” He nodded at the cracked plaster above the furnace room door, where ivy had rooted in the damp and now curled like a fist.  

The tenants’ association meeting had convened in the laundry room, as it did every third Thursday. Mrs. Peet had chaired these gatherings for seventeen years, ever since her husband died and the building’s caretaker duties passed to her. The ring box—a relic from the 1913 World’s Fair, won by the first caretaker in a raffle—sat at the center of every vote. Its rule was simple: whoever felt most responsible for the outcome kept it until the next decision.  

“It’s the children’s Christmas tree party versus your leaky gutters, Halvorsen,” snapped Mrs. Okoro, the second-floor seamstress, cradling her teacup. Her hands trembled; Mrs. Peet knew the medication that kept Mrs. Okoro’s arthritis at bay had been cut from the budget last month.  

Mrs. Peet’s chest tightened. She’d secretly rerouted maintenance funds to cover Mrs. Okoro’s treatment for three months now, siphoning from the light bill and the mouse-catching fund. But the deception pricked her. She’d always believed the box favored those who acted without selfishness. Yet here she was, hoarding responsibility like a bad debt.  

“Vote,” said Mr. Halvorsen.  

Hands rose. Mrs. Peet counted: six for triage, five for equality. The box grew cold in her hand. She should pass it to Mr. Halvorsen, but her fingers stuck. A trick of the metal, she told herself. Or guilt.  

That night, Mrs. Peet sat on the fire escape, the box in her lap. Below, the shared garden—once her pride, now a tangle of volunteer sunflowers and wilted lettuce—buzzed with moths. Her nephew Leo, who managed the building’s books in exchange for a basement flat, joined her with two mugs of cocoa.  

“You kept the box again,” he said. It wasn’t a question.  

“I couldn’t let Mrs. Okoro lose her hands.”  

“You mean you couldn’t lose her.” Leo’s voice softened. “She’s the only one who still calls you ‘dear.’”  

Mrs. Peet swatted his arm. “Don’t be lurid.”  

“But I’m right.” He leaned against the railing. “You know the box isn’t magic, right? It’s just a fancy paperweight. People keep it because they want to feel chosen.”  

“Then why’s it always warm when I hold it?”  

“Arthritis,” Leo deadpanned. “Or you’re just the only one honest enough to admit you like being in charge.”  

The next morning, Mrs. Peet found the east wing’s ceiling sagging like a waterbed. Mrs. Okoro stood beneath it, measuring for new curtains.  

“You shouldn’t be here,” Mrs. Peet warned.  

“Neither should you,” Mrs. Okoro said, calm. “But here we are.” She held up the ring box. It gleamed, daisies blooming bright. “Found it on the floor. Must’ve dropped it.”  

Mrs. Peet’s stomach dropped. The box never fell.  

At the next meeting, Mrs. Okoro chaired. The box rested in her hands, warm and still. They voted to patch the east wing, diverting funds from the gardens. Mrs. Peet said nothing.  

Later, Leo found her pruning the sunflowers, now doomed.  

“She’s got a point,” he said. “You can’t save everyone.”  

“No,” Mrs. Peet agreed. She handed him a flower. Its petals were already curling shut. “But I can make them think they had a choice.”  

The box, meanwhile, had started to sprout. Tiny green shoots pushed through its engraving, as if the daisies had decided to grow where they were needed most.
