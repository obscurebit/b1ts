# The Scale in the Cedar Shed

“You’re *still* using that thing?” Marjorie’s voice cracks like the dry summer air. She looms over the cedar shed, her apron stiff with flour, pointing at the scale as if it’s a dead animal.  

You tighten the bolt on its rusted leg. “It’s calibrated,” you say, though your thumb still bears the bruise from yesterday’s miscalibration. The scale’s pan shivers, dented from when Hank’s boy dropped a cinderblock on it.  

“Calibrated to *lie*,” Marjorie spits. “My sugar ration came up ‘three pounds short.’ By *your* machine.”  

The shed smells of sawdust and gasoline. Behind Marjorie, the line of neighbors shifts. Mrs. Peet from number twelve fidgets with her hearing aid, the one you fixed last winter when her grandson yanked its wires out. You want to tell them all to go home, that the scale’s been fighting you for months, its numbers slipping like wet ink. But Debbie’s there, her arms full of cabbage from her victory garden, watching you with that look—like she’s memorizing the shape of your apologies.  

You met Debbie when the scale first broke. She brought you lemonade while you pried open its guts, her fingers brushing yours as she handed over the glass. Three years now, and you’ve never figured out if she stays because of the scale or in spite of it.  

---  

The cedar shed creaks in the wind. Inside, the scale’s mechanism hums, a sound like a trapped wasp. You’d retired it years ago, but then the rationing started, and people needed a number to trust. So you oiled its joints, straightened its pan, and let them think it worked.  

Most days, you tweak the readings yourself. A nudge here, a subtraction there. Marjorie gets her sugar; Mrs. Peet’s hearing aid stays funded. The scale’s lies keep the neighborhood balanced, like a teetering shelf.  

But today, a man in a gray suit appears at the shed. No dust on his shoes. No name you recognize. He holds a clipboard, the paper pristine.  

“Regulation 14-B,” he says. “All communal measurement devices must adhere to federal standards.” His finger taps the scale’s cracked face. “This is substandard.”  

You laugh, but it comes out a wheeze. “It’s the only one we’ve got.”  

“Then you’ll follow procedure.” He thrusts the clipboard at you. “Daily audits. Public corrections. No… adjustments.” His eyes flick to the wrench in your pocket.  

---  

Rain softens the streets by morning. The line outside the shed is shorter. Marjorie’s there, but she won’t meet your eyes. Debbie stands at the back, her cabbage bundled tight.  

The man in gray watches as you test the scale. A five-pound weight wobbles on its pan. The needle jerks, settles on *4.2*. You write it down, per the rules.  

“See?” the man says to the crowd. “Transparency.”  

But Mrs. Peet’s ration slips from *two pounds of flour* to *one and a half*. She starts to cry. Debbie steps forward, but you shake your head. *Not yet*, you mouth.  

At dusk, you find the man at the diner, eating pie. You slide into the booth, your hands smudged with carbon paper.  

“They’re going to starve,” you say.  

He doesn’t look up. “The system corrects itself.”  

You think of Debbie’s garden, how she grows extra tomatoes for the widow next door. How Marjorie trades sugar for Mrs. Peet’s mending. The scale’s job was never to measure weight.  

You reach into your coat, pull out the wrench. Place it on the table.  

“Here,” you say. “Audit this.”  

---  

The next morning, the shed is empty. The scale’s gone.  

Marjorie’s at the end of the line, arguing with Debbie about whose turn it is to keep the ledger. The man in gray hasn’t returned.  

You stand at the front, holding a length of clothesline.  

“New rule,” you say. “We weigh by what we share.”  

Mrs. Peet nods, handing her flour to the widow. Debbie smiles, tucking a tomato into your pocket.  

The line moves, slow and steady, as if it’s always been this way.
