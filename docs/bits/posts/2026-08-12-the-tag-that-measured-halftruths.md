# The Tag That Measured Half-Truths

You’re halfway through your lunch break when the tag on Mrs. Okoro’s collar starts blinking crimson, and she says, “Don’t tell me it’s *another* error,” like you’re the one who broke the system instead of just the one stuck maintaining it.  

The counter smells like stale naan and institutional disinfectant. You lean over the glass partition, squinting at the tag’s flickering display. “It says here you logged 47 emotional units at the community potluck. The audit says 22.”  

Mrs. Okoro snorts. “The audit’s wrong. I spent three hours smiling at that insufferable man from the water board. You think I do this for *fun*?”  

You don’t tell her your mother’s tag once glitched so badly it credited her 300 units for a single dinner party. You don’t say how you wiped the record before anyone noticed, how your mother still denies the numbers were ever off.  

---  

Two years ago, at your aunt’s funeral, your cousin Jabari gripped your shoulder and said, “You’ll handle the reconciliation, right? Your mom’s… fragile.” As if fragility explained why she’d leased half her grief to a stranger named Darren in exchange for grocery credits. As if it wasn’t your job to clean up the mess.  

Now, Mrs. Okoro leans in, her voice lowering. “I heard they’re making the tags non-editable next quarter. Centralized logging. No more ‘discrepancies.’” Her fingers drum the counter, a rhythm only she understands. “Your mother still coming in for her weekly adjustments?”  

You freeze. The question is a knife. Everyone knows your mother’s name in the ledger, how her revised memories keep blooming like mold. But Mrs. Okoro doesn’t know *you* erased the record of her leasing last month’s guilt over Jabari’s eviction to a tech contractor. Doesn’t know you’re the reason her tag still shows a zero balance.  

---  

Back in the present, Jessa, the intern, sidles up beside you with a tray of reheated samosas. “You’re letting her walk all over you,” she mutters, her voice honeyed with the kind of confidence that comes from not having a family name tied to every emotional ledger in the district. “Tell her to come back during actual hours.”  

But Mrs. Okoro is already striding out, her crimson tag flashing like a warning. Jessa watches her go, then turns on you. “You ever think maybe your mom’s the reason they’re locking the system down? All that revising… it’s like she’s stealing from the rest of us.”  

You want to snap, but your throat tightens. Last week, your mother sat across from you at this very counter, her tag blinking green for “resolved.” “I don’t remember leasing anything,” she said, sipping chai. “But the numbers look good, don’t they?”  

You didn’t tell her about the man who came in screaming about a debt he couldn’t pay, how his tag had been drained by someone using your mother’s old ID. You just adjusted his balance and told him it was a glitch.  

---  

At day’s end, you find a note tucked under your door: *Meeting in the break room. –Jassa.*  

She’s there with a printout, her smirk sharp as a scalpel. “I cross-referenced the leaked audits. Your mom’s tag? It’s been synced to a ghost account. Someone’s been transferring her emotional units to a shell company.” She slides the paper across the table. “Darren Kowalski. Ring a bell?”  

It does. The man from the water board. The one Mrs. Okoro hated.  

“You could report it,” Jessa says. “Or you could keep playing librarian for your mother’s lies.”  

The break room smells like burnt coffee and desperation. You think of your mother’s face when she said, “I don’t remember,” and how you almost believed her.  

---  

The next morning, you reprogram the system to flag any tag synced to a shell account. Let them all come screaming. Let the ledger burn.  

Mrs. Okoro returns at noon, her tag now a steady green. “Fixed?” you ask.  

She nods, but her eyes linger on the bruise-colored smudge under your chin—the one you got when you smashed your mother’s tag against the sink last night, trying to make it stop blinking.  

“You okay?” she asks.  

You hand her a receipt. “Paid in full.”  

The lie tastes like disinfectant.
