# The Tagging of F. Norris Henshaw

The first time the garment tags started rearranging themselves, I was already thirty minutes late for my shift at the County Records Annex, thanks to my brother Jace’s latest “emergency”—a flat tire on his Pontiac Firebird, which he’d somehow managed to park in a no-loading zone outside the laundromat. Again. I’d spent the morning haggling with a tow truck driver who refused to accept anything but cash, then lied to our supervisor, Marv, about a “family dental emergency” to cover Jace’s absence.  

Marv, of course, knew. Everyone knew. Jace hadn’t submitted a single marriage license application since the Clinton administration, yet he still showed up every Tuesday with a bag of doughnuts and a story about his “back trouble.” The tags, though—those were new.  

They lived in the basement vault, hundreds of yellowed cotton strips stapled to 19th-century work orders, each inked with a number and a name: *F. Norris Henshaw, Tailor’s Assistant (Men’s Undergarments)*; *M. Delaney, Corset Fitter (Ladies’ Stay Repairs)*. The numbers changed. Not the names, never the names, but the numbers—sometimes jumping ahead, sometimes looping back. Last week, 47 became 12. The week before, 89 split into 33 and 34. Marv thought it was humidity. I thought it was the same magic that made Jace’s parking tickets disappear.  

“It’s a union thing,” said Tanya, our coworker who’d once glued her ID badge to her forehead during a labor dispute and refused to talk about it. She was stapling tags to a stack of microfiche sleeves, her hair the color of a highlighter cap. “The numbers reflect the city’s moral debt. Obviously.”  

I didn’t argue. Tanya believed the flu was a capitalist construct and kept a jar of pickled okra on her desk “for grounding.” But when the tags started spelling out coordinates—42.8852° N, 85.6705° W—on a Tuesday, I realized the debt wasn’t metaphorical.  

That was the day Jace showed up, contrite and reeking of bourbon, with a manila envelope addressed to *F. Norris Henshaw*. “Found it in the attic,” he said. “Granddad’s stuff. Probably worthless.” Inside was a ledger page, the same spidery ink as the tags, detailing a “Night Audit of Moral Ledgers” from 1897. The numbers matched the vault’s tags.  

Tanya and I met in the parking lot during lunch, swapping Slim Jims and theories. “The tags are a key,” she said, ignoring the mustard stain on her blouse. “The coordinates are the lock. The city’s been hiding debt settlements in plain sight.” She gestured to the microfiche machine, its screen flickering like a dying TV. “Ever notice how the sewer bills always go up after a tag flips?”  

I hadn’t. But I had noticed Jace’s envelope included a receipt for $12,000 in “Moral Adjustment Fees” paid by the city to a P. Norris Henshaw in 1923. My grandfather. The same one who’d supposedly won the family a lifetime supply of thread in a poker game.  

That night, I stayed late, feeding tags into the microfiche machine. Each one spat out a page from the 1897 ledger, detailing bribes, favors, and one very confused entry about a “hydra-headed goose” that terrorized the city’s aldermen. The coordinates led to a manhole cover behind the laundromat, where Jace was waiting, sweating through his Eagles t-shirt.  

“You dug it up,” I said. The envelope was in his hand.  

“He owed me,” Jace said, which was his refrain for every loan, every favor, every half-truth he’d ever told. “The tags started changing after Granddad died. I thought… maybe it was money. Maybe it was *mine*.”  

The manhole cover groaned when I pried it open. Below was a vault, not of gold or secrets, but of plastic shopping bags—thousands, each stuffed with receipts, tags, and microfiche, all documenting a century of the city’s quiet graft. A mouse scurried through a bag from a long-closed five-and-dime.  

“You’re an idiot,” I told Jace, tossing him a bag. “This isn’t treasure. It’s evidence.”  

He blinked. “So… we take it to the *Tribune*?”  

I thought of Marv’s impending retirement, Tanya’s pickled okra, the way the tags never changed on days when no one lied. “No,” I said. “We burn it.”  

Jace looked horrified. “But the *tags*—”  

“Are just tags,” I said. And for once, I meant it.  

The next morning, the *Tribune* ran a story about a “mysterious fire” in the records annex basement. Marv retired early. Tanya started a rumor about a curse. Jace got a job at the laundromat, folding towels and occasionally disappearing into the alley with a manila envelope.  

I kept one tag. Number 47, now 12. It hangs on my desk, next to the family photo where Jace’s toothy grin dominates the frame. Sometimes, when the phone rings too long or a coworker complains about the air conditioning, the number flickers. Just for a second.  

Then it settles.  

Like it’s waiting.  

But I never ask why.  

Not anymore.
