# The Responsibility Spoon

Mara bent over the atrium’s central fountain, screwing the casing back onto the water recycler. A man’s voice hissed behind her: “Fix it so the spoon’s mine. Now.”  

She didn’t look up. “The fountain’s got a leak in its feedback valve. If you want it to stop smelling like iron, you’ll need to—”  

“I’m here about the spoon.” The man’s shoes, too shiny for the building’s cracked tiles, edged closer. “My daughter’s at the clinic. They won’t let her eat unless it’s officially hers. Just reroute the ownership log.”  

Mara finally turned. It was Jalen, from 4B, who’d once traded her a working voltage regulator for a jar of her mother’s pickled chilies. His hands trembled. “The system’s got a 48-hour grief delay,” she said. “You can’t bypass it without a committee stamp.”  

“I brought the stamp.” He thrust a frayed cloth pouch under her chin. Inside, the inkpad squelched. “Use it. I’ll owe you.”  

She stepped back. “I’m not a notary.”  

“Then be a neighbor.” His voice cracked. “They’re starving her, Mara. The clinic says she’s ‘unowned’ until the spoon chooses again.”  

The spoon in question hung from a cord around Jalen’s neck, its bowl clouded with old soup stains. Every resident had one, assigned at the building’s last Reconciliation. The spoons didn’t choose; they *remembered* who’d last fed someone else. A week ago, Jalen’s wife had stopped feeding their daughter, then herself. The spoon had migrated to Mara during the cleanup, warm against her throat as she scrubbed biohazard stickers off doorframes.  

“Mara.” Her brother’s voice buzzed in her earpiece. “Mom’s asking why the spoons in the east wing are all singing.”  

“Tell her it’s a glitch,” Mara muttered, though she knew better. The spoons hummed when ownership was contested.  

Jalen’s fist closed around the spoon. “I’ll tell them you stole it. From the clinic. From my wife’s *corpse*.”  

Mara’s throat tightened. The spoons didn’t leave the dead until someone else took responsibility. Jalen’s wife had lain untouched for hours, her spoon dull and cold, until Mara had lifted it, feeling the weight of the act like a stone in her chest.  

“Fine,” she said. “But we do it right. Bring your daughter’s care number and a receipt for her last meal.”  

---  

The building’s central office smelled of lemon disinfectant and burnt toast. Mara’s brother, Eli, sat cross-legged on the floor, sorting physical receipts into piles. “Mom’s in the west lounge,” he said. “She’s… performing.”  

Mara winced. Their mother’s “performances”—rehearsed monologues about her past as a data ethicist—had grown more frequent since the Reconciliation. Since the day the spoons started migrating, exposing who had stopped caring for whom.  

A receipt fluttered down. Eli snatched it midair. “This one’s from the flower vendor. Smells like guilt.”  

“Burn it,” Mara said. “Or don’t. Just keep the system balanced.”  

Eli’s smile didn’t reach his eyes. “You know the new policy? If a spoon hasn’t moved in 24 hours, they fine the whole floor.”  

Jalen appeared, clutching a girl with eyes like smudged pencil marks. Mara took the receipt he offered: *1 bowl lentil stew, 1 cup water, 1 vitamin tablet. Paid in community credits.* Standard clinic fare.  

The spoon around Jalen’s neck shivered. Mara pressed her palm to it, feeling the ghost of his wife’s neglect, the daughter’s hunger. Her own spoon, tucked under her shirt, grew warm in sympathy.  

“I can’t just forge a transfer,” she said.  

“You already did.” Eli tossed her a charred receipt. “For the Varners’ kid. And the woman in 7C. And—”  

“Those were emergencies.”  

“This *is* an emergency.” Jalen’s daughter clutched his leg. Her spoon, dangling from a shoelace, began to hum.  

Mara’s earpiece buzzed. “Mara,” her mother’s voice crackled, “they’re asking about the spoons. Tell them it’s a metaphor. For accountability. For *atonement*.”  

The girl’s spoon sang louder.  

---  

At dusk, Mara found her mother in the west lounge, addressing an empty room. “The spoon,” her mother declared to no one, “is the only honest bureaucrat. It cannot be bribed. It cannot be fooled.”  

Mara leaned against the doorframe. “Jalen’s daughter hasn’t eaten in two days.”  

Her mother turned, lips pursed. “Then the spoon will find her a new caregiver. That’s the process.”  

“The process is broken.”  

“No.” Her mother’s voice hardened. “*You* are broken. You think you’re preserving something, but you’re just… delaying the reckoning.”  

The girl’s spoon wailed in the distance.  

---  

Mara stood before the committee at dawn, Jalen’s receipt in hand. The chair, a woman with a spoon dangling from her earlobe, waved a hand. “Approve the transfer.”  

Mara didn’t move. “The system’s designed to reflect care, not fabricate it.”  

“You’re refusing?”  

“Yes.”  

The room’s spoons fell silent.  

Jalen’s daughter, waiting outside, clutched her spoon to her chest. It glowed faintly, as if considering her.  

Mara walked away, her own spoon cold against her skin for the first time in weeks.  

---  

That night, Eli found her in the maintenance closet. “They froze your credits,” he said. “And Mom’s performance got a standing ovation.”  

Mara tightened a bolt on the water recycler. “Good.”  

“You’re going to let them starve her?”  

“No.” She stood, wiping her hands. “I’m going to make the spoon choose.”  

He blinked. “How?”  

“By feeding her myself.”  

The spoons, after all, only ever followed the last act of care.
