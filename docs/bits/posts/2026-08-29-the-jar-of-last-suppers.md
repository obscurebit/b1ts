# The Jar of Last Suppers

The smell of microwaved soy sauce and burnt rice clung to the walls of the visa processing office, a greasy mist that settled on the shoulders of the applicants waiting in folding chairs. Mira adjusted her headset, the earpiece still warm from the previous interpreter, and nodded at the camera in the corner. It blinked red. Recording.  

Room 3B was always the coldest. The table between Mira and the applicants had a crack running down its center, filled with what looked like fossilized sesame seeds. Today’s case: a family from Hubei province, their asylum claim hinging on a video of a demolished apartment complex. The father’s hands trembled as he pushed the phone across the table. “You see?” he said. “Our home is gone. We cannot return.”  

Mira glanced at the jar on the shelf behind them. The jar her predecessor had left, its mouth sealed with a lid that never stuck no matter how many times they scrubbed it. Every recorded testimony went into it—not the words spoken, but the version everyone agreed to perform. The jar’s shoulder was etched with a character that glowed faintly when a story held.  

The father’s video showed a rubble-strewn lot, but Mira noticed the date stamp: three months after the family’s alleged flight. She’d seen this before. The system was phasing out human interpreters, replacing them with AIs that couldn’t smell the lie in a man’s sweat or see the way his wife avoided the camera. But the jar would hold this version, the one where the family’s fear was enough.  

Her sister, Lin, waited outside. Again. Mira could see her through the door’s glass pane, slumped in a chair with a frayed *Wait Here* sign taped to the wall above her. Lin’s hands were clasped too neatly, her posture too straight—a performance for the security camera. They’d agreed: today was the last day Mira would “misunderstand” Lin’s texts about the money they owed their cousin. The money Lin actually had, hidden in a shoebox under her bed.  

“You’re sure the jar won’t… contradict?” the mother asked now, her voice brittle.  

Mira touched the jar’s cool glass. It hummed, recognizing her. “It records what we agree is true,” she said.  

At lunch, Mira found Lin eating instant noodles in the courtyard. The financial detail she’d memorized—a money order for $450.00, the amount needed to renew Lin’s visa—burned in her pocket. “They approved the family,” Mira said, sitting. “The jar accepted their story.”  

Lin didn’t look up. “And us?”  

“The system’s changing. They’ll let us go soon.”  

“But you’ll stay useful.” Lin’s chopsticks clacked against the styrofoam. “That’s what you want.”  

Mira’s throat tightened. The jar had started to reject recent testimonies, the glow fading mid-sentence. She’d taken to tampering with the recordings, adding pauses, softening inflections. But the AI auditors were catching on.  

Back in Room 3B, the camera blinked. A new applicant: a teenager with a chipped phone and a drawing of a house with two smokestacks. “My father sent this,” she said. “From the refugee camp.”  

Mira reached for the jar, but Lin’s voice hissed in her memory: *You want to be needed so badly you’ll erase yourself.*  

The jar’s character flickered. Mira hesitated. The girl’s story was clean, no discrepancies. But the system was redesigning them out, one verified truth at a time.  

She closed the jar’s lid without recording.  

“No,” she said. “I won’t translate this.”  

The camera blinked red. The girl stared.  

Mira stood, the jar cradled against her chest. For once, she wouldn’t be the bridge.  

The girl’s hands curled into fists. “My father—”  

“Is not here,” Mira said.  

Outside, Lin was gone. On the floor, a sticky note: *I sent the money myself. Don’t refuse too long.*  

Mira let the door swing shut.  

The jar’s glow pulsed, uncertain.  

She refused to open it.
