### The Scale in the Hall

The first time the scale lied, I was fetching linens from Storage B. The door jammed, as always, and when I yanked it open, a moth the size of a postcard fluttered out, its wings dusted with the same yellow lint as the blankets. I didn’t note it in the ledger. Moths weren’t on the form.  

By the time I reached the hall, Mrs. Halvorsen was shouting. Her voice carried the Scandinavian clip that meant she’d been speaking for some time. “It said 68 kilos yesterday! Now it says 52! My bones haven’t evaporated!” The scale, a corroded hospital model with a cracked glass window, stood between her and her son, who slouched against the radiator, peeling labels off a jar of pickled herring.  

I knelt to check the wiring. The scale’s needle quivered at 52.3. Mrs. Halvorsen’s son flicked a label at me. “It’s broken. Like everything here.”  

“Like your teeth,” Mrs. Halvorsen snapped.  

The scale had begun its career in a maternity ward, then served a fish market, then us—a refugee hostel where the only constant was the draft through the windows. The city’s health inspector called it a “heritage appliance.” I called it a liar. But the rules were clear: allocations of blankets, food, even heating credits depended on its readings.  

That night, I stayed late. The scale’s logbook showed a pattern: weights dropped when a crowd gathered, soared when someone wept. Consensus, not gravity, governed it. I thought of Dr. Varga, who’d set up the system before vanishing mid-shift one Tuesday. “The body is a story,” she used to say, “and the story changes.”  

I didn’t want to know what that meant. But the next morning, when Mr. Chen accused the scale of stealing his wife’s mass—“She’s not *that* thin!”—I did what Dr. Varga would’ve done. I invited the hall to gather. Twenty people crammed in, arguing. The needle climbed. Mrs. Halvorsen clapped. Mr. Chen wept. The scale settled on 63.4.  

I wrote it down.  

Years later, they’ll ask why I stayed. Why I didn’t trash the scale, demand a new one. But the hostel ran on complicity. The city loved the numbers we produced. The residents loved the warmth of a crowd-pleasing weight. And me? I loved the way the scale hummed when it lied, a sound like a cello string being sawed by a paperclip.  

The last entry I made was for a girl who never arrived. Her paperwork came first: *Height: 145 cm. Weight: ?* The scale was empty. I wrote 45.0. The social worker nodded. The girl never came. Maybe she was better off elsewhere.  

I left the logbook open on Dr. Varga’s old desk. The moth from Storage B rested on the page, wings folded. The needle trembled at 0.0.  

Consensus, I suppose, is a kind of physics.
