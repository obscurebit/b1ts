### The Tag That Bled Ink

The tag bled ink in precise swirls, each droplet a future citation. I pressed the fabric between my thumbs, watching crimson letters form: *Unverified. Expired. Fraudulent.* The machine hummed, spitting out a paper slip that hadn’t happened yet.  

“Late again, Mrs. Halvorsen,” I said, though the name wasn’t mine. The woman standing across the counter—real name Liora, according to her counterfeit ID—flinched. Her son’s school uniform tag had started oozing three days ago. She’d waited too long to bring it in.  

“Bus,” she muttered, clutching a fraying umbrella. Rain speckled the linoleum behind her.  

I didn’t care. The machine cared. Its needle pricked the tag, sucking out the prophecy. Paper fed from the roll, smudged with tomorrow’s shame. Liora’s son would be expelled in 4.3 days unless the tag dried up. I stapled the warning to her receipt.  

“Fifty credits,” I said.  

She paid in crumpled notes. The supervisor, Mr. Vex, watched from his glass booth, his tie knotted too tight. He’d been counting the seconds since her arrival. Tardiness was a moral failing. He tapped his ledger, the pages fluffed with future failures.  

At lunch, I ate stale rye crackers in the break room, where the vending machine sold aspirin and hope. My daughter’s tag bled quietly in my pocket. I’d forged it myself, using the backroom press. She’d gotten into the tech academy, but the ink would start leaking next month. By then, I’d need better credentials—or a better forgery.  

Mr. Vex appeared, his shadow swallowing the doorway. “You delayed the process, Mara.”  

“The machine was calibrating.”  

“Your crackers are noisy.”  

I crunched louder. He hated that. His own tag, stitched into his blazer, had bled through his shirt this morning. A brown star over his heart. He’d never admit it.  

The afternoon line stretched into the hall. A boy’s tag wept black ink, predicting a factory accident. His mother argued with the slip, as if the paper had lied. I stamped *URGENT* on her form, though the machine didn’t care.  

At dusk, Mr. Vex lingered. “You know why I can’t let you quit.”  

I did. His brother ran the tag-printing union. My sister managed the ink factory. We were all sewn into the same rot.  

He pressed a tag into my hand—his brother’s, fresh off the press. “No bleed. Perfect forgery.”  

I rolled it between my fingers. “Why give it to me?”  

“Your girl’s tag… it’s failing.” He looked away. “Mine too. The real ones always do.”  

The confession hung, sharp as the machine’s needle. He wasn’t threatening me. He was begging.  

I nodded. “Fifty credits.”  

He paid.  

That night, I swapped my daughter’s tag with his brother’s fake. The ink stayed dry.  

At home, I counted my pay: 298 credits. The machine had taken two as its cut. I taped the receipt to the wall, where dozens of slips curled like dead leaves. Each one a future someone had survived, or not.  

Mr. Vex’s tag bled through his report the next week. I stamped it *APPROVED* and handed him the slip.  

He read it, then me. “We’re all just mending the same rotten fabric,” he said.  

The line inched forward. The machine hummed.  

I nodded. “Pass me the needle.”
